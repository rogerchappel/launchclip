# Jev scores rubrics, not audience response

"JEV" is almost certainly **Jev, the first "System One" model from TypeSafe AI**: a hosted, closed-weight, non-generative model released in early access on 15 September 2026 that takes a "state" plus typed questions and returns scores and probabilities, never text ([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). The identification is about 90 to 95% confident; only the person who asked can confirm it. It is fast and cheap: independent tests measure roughly 0.15 to 0.44 seconds per call and a few cents per thousand judgments, at a reported **$0.042 per million input tokens with output free** ([JEV-as-a-Judge](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.26550.md), [LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith)). It is API-only and proprietary, so it cannot ship inside an open-source project or run on a Mac; local use means an open look-alike, which runs on a 16 GB Apple Silicon laptop but is less accurate and uncalibrated ([Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/en/articles/openjev-non-generative-ai-alternatives/), [jev-on-a-laptop](https://github.com/rorshopping/jev-on-a-laptop)). The accuracy evidence is good for one job and absent for another: Jev is near a frontier LLM judge when the verdict can be read off the text, and at chance when asked to judge prose quality cold, as is every other judge tested ([JEV-as-a-Judge](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.26550.md)). **No source found links Jev's scores to views, retention, click-through or conversions.** For the content engine, that makes Jev (or a local equivalent) a cheap wide filter, tagger and gate over thousands of candidates, with live performance data as the only judge of what works. The strongest design the research supports is: generate many, gate and tag cheaply, keep the shortlist diverse, and let real results from posted content correct the scores.

## A two-month-old closed model that returns probabilities instead of prose

TypeSafe AI came out of two years in stealth on 15 September 2026 with $40M in seed funding; its founder, Diogo Almeida, is an ex-OpenAI researcher credited as a co-inventor of RLHF ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/a/5296711), [Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2026/09/jev-explained/)). "Jev" is not an acronym. It is named after the economist William Stanley Jevons, and "System One" borrows Kahneman's term for fast, intuitive thinking ([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). A Carnegie Mellon paper writes the name in capitals, which is likely where "JEV" comes from ([JEV-as-a-Judge](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.26550.md)). The current version is Jev 1.14, available from 14 October 2026 ([TypeSafe models docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)).

The caller sends a `state` (text, JSON, or PNG/JPEG images) and a map of named questions, and gets typed answers back. There are four answer types: **Choice** (one of up to 255 options, with a probability per option), **Score** (a position on a caller-defined ordered scale of 2 to 10 levels), **Noul** (the probability that a yes/no statement is true), and **Box** (a bounding box in an image, added in 1.14) ([TypeSafe System One docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/concepts/system-one), [Requesty](https://www.requesty.ai/blog/typesafe-jev-explained)). Jev has no built-in idea of "good content". It scores whatever rubric the caller writes in plain language. It cannot explain an answer, and there is no customer fine-tuning: the same weights serve everyone, steered only by the state, instructions and criteria in the request ([TypeSafe models docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)). Audio and video are not supported, so a video has to be reduced to a transcript and sampled frames first ([TypeSafe models docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md)).

How it works inside is mostly undisclosed. TypeSafe claims a new architecture with a "parallel sampler" and a training method called RLCD (Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions), and publishes no detail on either, nor on parameter count or training data ([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). The best outside account is that it reads the input once and takes a "restricted softmax" over only the declared answer options in a single forward pass, with no token-by-token generation; one tutorial reproduced the idea with a small open Qwen model ([Hyperstack](https://www.hyperstack.cloud/technical-resources/tutorials/jev-inside-typesafe-ais-first-system-one-decision-model)). A sceptical engineer argues "System One" is branding, that the capability comes mainly from the base model, and that others built Jev-compatible APIs from open models with similar latency ([Lee Han Chung](https://leehanchung.github.io/blogs/2026/09/20/jev-return-ai-ml-engineering/)). The practical reading is that Jev is a language model whose output is read as probabilities over a fixed label set. TypeSafe has not confirmed that.

The same category now has larger entrants. Google's Gemini Decision 1, launched 11 November 2026, answers up to 1,000 questions per request, accepts video and audio directly, and costs $0.10 per million input tokens ([Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/gemini-decisions-api/)). An advertiser-focused blog also reports an OpenAI "Decisions API" in limited preview ([Bir.ch](https://bir.ch/blog/jev-guide-for-advertisers)). The pattern matters more than the vendor, which argues for a scorer interface in the content engine rather than a Jev integration.

## Thousands of judgments cost cents, but not on a Mac

Speed comes from skipping generation and from packing many questions against one state into a single request. TypeSafe's docs say adding questions "barely changes the response time" ([TypeSafe docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai)). The vendor claims 70 to 500 ms per call and "193.6x faster, 444.6x cheaper" than frontier LLMs ([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev)). Independent numbers are smaller but still large. The table below shows what outside testers measured.

| Source | Jev latency | Jev cost | Compared with |
|---|---|---|---|
| [JEV-as-a-Judge (CMU)](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.26550.md) | 0.15 s median | $0.044 per 1,000 judgments | GPT-6: 1.89 s, $12.18 (about 13x faster, 277x cheaper) |
| [LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith) | 0.44 s average | $0.00035 per call | Three LLM judges: 2.16 to 2.83 s |
| [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/tutorials/jev-vs-llm-as-a-judge/) | 168 to 171 ms median | $0.021 to $0.043 per 1,000 | An LLM judge: 1.7 to 2.9 s, $0.114 to $0.261 (about 5 to 6x cheaper) |

The size of the gap depends on the baseline. Against a frontier reasoning model it is hundreds of times cheaper; against a cheaper LLM judge it is five or six times; and one search company found Jev cost materially more per document than its own trained rerankers ([Parallel](https://parallel.ai/blog/testing-jev.md)). "Thousands at a time" in practice means thousands of concurrent requests finishing in tens of seconds. User-reported runs include 6,245 companies in 25 seconds for $0.37 and 4,367 ads tagged in 39 seconds for $0.27 ([Analytics Vidhya](https://www.analyticsvidhya.com/blog/2026/09/jev-explained/), [Bir.ch](https://bir.ch/blog/jev-guide-for-advertisers)). These are single unreproduced posts, and the most viral one was reportedly copied word for word by three accounts ([Bir.ch](https://bir.ch/blog/jev-guide-for-advertisers)). There is no official throughput figure and no documented batch endpoint; third-party rate-limit numbers conflict with each other ([docs index](https://docs.typesafe.ai/llms.txt), [Layer3 Labs](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/jev-limits)). At the reported price, scoring 10,000 short content items costs well under a dollar.

Several availability facts are unsettled. The price comes from third-party write-ups and the vendor's launch post; the official docs index has no pricing page, and TypeSafe says it cannot prove the price is not subsidised ([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), [docs index](https://docs.typesafe.ai/llms.txt)). Sources disagree on whether sign-up is open to everyone or still waitlisted ([Layer3 Labs](https://www.layer3labs.io/guides/jev-limits)). Context limits are reported as 64k tokens per request and 32k per question in TypeSafe's models docs, and as 32,000 tokens on Cloudflare's model page ([TypeSafe models docs](https://docs.typesafe.ai/models.md), [Cloudflare](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ai/models/typesafe/jev/)). Cloudflare also labels image input as beta and not recommended for production.

**The licence is the hard constraint for a local-first engine.** Jev's weights are unpublished and there is no self-hosting ([Hyperstack](https://www.hyperstack.cloud/technical-resources/tutorials/jev-inside-typesafe-ais-first-system-one-decision-model)). It is served through TypeSafe's own endpoint, Cloudflare, Vercel, OpenRouter and Requesty, and is not on the three major clouds ([Requesty](https://www.requesty.ai/blog/typesafe-jev-explained)). An open-source tool can only call it with the user's own key. The Master Customer Agreement was not read during this research, so commercial-use and output-use terms are unconfirmed. One unverified secondary source claims a clause forbids using outputs to develop a similar product, which would cloud the legal status of any clone distilled from Jev's answers.

Local use on macOS means an open look-alike. The trick is portable: prompt a local model once, read its next-token probabilities over the fixed options, and batch one row per question. Three options stand out.

| Local option | Licence | Mac evidence | Accuracy evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| [jev-on-a-laptop](https://github.com/rorshopping/jev-on-a-laptop) (MLX, 4-bit Qwen) | MIT code, Apache-2.0 engine | 16 GB M5 MacBook Air: 28 fields in 0.41 s (1.5B) to 1.52 s (7B) | 7B agreed with the reference 73.8% vs Jev's 86.6% on 343 question pairs; over 0.90 confident on 13 of its 20 wrong fields |
| [open-jev](https://github.com/daseinlabs/open-jev) (MLX, Gemma 3 4B) | MIT code; Gemma licence for the model | M5 Pro 64 GB: 0.17 s per 8-option decision with context cached; about 8 GB memory | Repo warns outputs are "softmaxed likelihoods, not calibrated judgements" |
| [autotrust/JEV-9B](https://huggingface.co/autotrust/JEV) (Qwen3.5-9B plus adapter) | Apache-2.0 weights, distilled from Jev's outputs | None; benchmarked only on a data-centre GPU (about 340 to 400 decisions per second) | 90.2% top-1 agreement with Jev on Choice; 11.5% of 16-option answers change with option order alone |

All of these numbers are the projects' own and unverified. A survey of seven such implementations found none that reproduces Jev's calibration training, and recommends starting with a stock model and changing only the readout ([Classmethod](https://dev.classmethod.jp/en/articles/openjev-non-generative-ai-alternatives/)). So a laptop clone scores thousands of items in minutes at no marginal cost, with weaker accuracy and overconfidence when wrong.

## Accurate when the answer is on the page, at chance on prose quality

The vendor's own evidence is thin. TypeSafe's headline evals use the averaged predictions of two frontier LLMs as the reference, not human labels, and on those Jev scores 67.8% against 73.1% and 74.1% for the two frontier models ([TypeSafe announcement](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), [Ryze](https://www.get-ryze.ai/blog/jev-for-marketing)). TypeSafe concedes its "0% hallucination" figure is true by design, because answers must match the schema, and is not a measure of correctness.

The best independent evidence is the Carnegie Mellon paper, which tests Jev against human-labelled judge benchmarks. Jev **ties GPT-6 on RewardBench at 92.5%** and is close on hallucination detection (87.3% vs 88.4%), but falls well behind where the verdict must be worked out: 78.6% vs 93.1% on JudgeBench and 68.4% vs 95.9% on logic puzzles ([JEV-as-a-Judge](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.26550.md)). Two results from that paper bear directly on content scoring. On reference-free prose judgments Jev scored 53.5% with an error-detection AUROC of 0.498, and the authors conclude "no tested judge is usable there". And where the worse answer is more elaborately written, Jev's accuracy drops to 76.6% against 90.1%. A cold "how good is this hook" score is the first case; a generator learning to please a fixed rubric is the second.

Jev's confidence is useful. At confidence 0.9 or higher it matches GPT-6 (94.7% vs 94.4%), and a cascade that accepts confident answers and escalates the rest beat the big judge alone at 41% of the cost ([JEV-as-a-Judge](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2609.26550.md)). On new workloads the same cascade escalated 74.2% of items, so the saving shrinks on unfamiliar tasks. Calibration is contested elsewhere: one critic reports failures on 7 of 8 datasets ([Lee Han Chung](https://leehanchung.github.io/blogs/2026/09/20/jev-return-ai-ml-engineering/)), and TypeSafe's own docs say Score outputs are "weakly calibrated numerically" and should be used for thresholds, not magnitudes ([TypeSafe docs, model jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness.md)). The same page lists unreliable counting and date comparison, a bias toward the first Choice option, falling accuracy as irrelevant state grows, and that injected instructions in the state can shift answers. LangChain's widely quoted 100% agreement result rests on five cases and one human labeller; its low run-to-run variance shows Jev is repeatable, not that it is right ([LangChain](https://www.langchain.com/blog/jev-agent-evals-langsmith)).

On summaries scored against expert ratings, Jev matched an LLM judge on coherence (0.42 vs 0.44 rank correlation) and trailed badly on consistency (0.47 vs 0.72), while on a closed faithfulness rubric it scored 95.5% ([OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/blog/tutorials/jev-vs-llm-as-a-judge/)). The pattern is consistent across sources: closed, written-down criteria on short evidence work; open quality judgments over long text do not.

## What Jev should not be trusted to do

**Jev should not be trusted to predict performance.** Documented marketing use is classification and triage, shown as speed-and-cost demos; an ad-tool blog that collected the runs notes that none reports click-through, return on ad spend or hold rate ([Bir.ch](https://bir.ch/blog/jev-guide-for-advertisers)). The wider literature sets the ceiling. Models trained on 120,651 real short videos, seeing the actual video and audio, reach a rank correlation of about 0.71 with five-second continuation; a text-only entry reached 0.441 ([VQualA 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.02969)). A zero-shot LLM ranking headlines against a publisher's A/B archive reached a rank correlation of 0.361 on the 399 tests with a reliable winner, 0.054 across all 1,695, and no signal at all on a second platform ([arXiv 2609.25010](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25010v1)). A text-only, zero-shot rubric scorer should be expected at or below the bottom of that range.

Four more limits follow from the evidence. It should not be the only judge of a rendered video, because it cannot watch one and the text-to-multimodal gap above is large. It should not be validated by comparing it with another model: in a study of 546 page A/B tests, judge models agreed with each other far more (kappa 0.74 to 0.88) than with outcomes (about 0.2), and a rule that always picks the changed variant scored 75 to 85% on curated libraries with zero decision value ([arXiv 2608.07517](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07517v1)). It should not power a persona panel: a 10-persona panel ranked headlines significantly worse than a single generic-reader prompt (0.084 vs 0.361) ([arXiv 2609.25010](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25010v1)). And it should not be handed untrusted web text with authority to act, since the state is an injection surface ([TypeSafe docs, model jaggedness](https://docs.typesafe.ai/model-jaggedness.md)).

Selecting the global top of thousands by one scorer also invites sameness. Frontier model output was measured as 66.2% more homogeneous than human writing, and restaurants with access to a chat model produced more similar social posts ([arXiv 2611.14828](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.14828), [SSRN 5367123](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5367123)). Both reached this research as search summaries only. No study was found on generator-versus-scorer gaming or novelty decay in marketing copy, so those risks are reasoned, not measured.

## Real results must correct every score

The method with the best evidence is to treat a model score as a prior and let live traffic overrule it. On 17,681 headline A/B tests, pure LLM prediction was only marginally better than random, but feeding the LLM predictor into an Upper Confidence Bound bandit beat standard A/B tests, pure bandits and pure-LLM approaches, "especially when experimental traffic is limited" ([LOLA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.02611)). That evidence is from publisher headlines; no bandit study on organic short-form posts by small creators was found.

The most defensible Jev-specific workflow is "tag, then join". Jev labels each piece with consistent features (hook type, angle, format, awareness stage), and plain code joins those tags to the brand's own performance numbers. One blog proposes this as a six-step loop and keeps a pattern only when it has at least 10 ads and 50 purchases behind it; the author did not run it end to end ([Shhots](https://shhots.ai/blog/jev-ai-meta-ads-mcp-creative-loop/)). The division of labour is sound: the scorer supplies features, the brand's data supplies the outcome.

Because Jev has no tuning path, brand adaptation lives outside the model, in four steps of rising cost. First, write the brand's known winning and losing traits into Score legends and yes/no questions. Second, place a few reference posts in the state; this is untested. Third, treat the vector of answers as features and fit a small regularised linear model against the brand's own outcomes. Fourth, let a bandit over live posts override all of it. The data requirement is real: only 23.5% of headline tests had a statistically reliable winner, and about 140 confident calls were estimated necessary for 80% power at a kappa of 0.30 ([arXiv 2609.25010](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.25010v1), [arXiv 2608.07517](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07517v1)). A brand with tens of posts cannot validate a scorer. Until it has low hundreds of posts with outcomes, scores are a tie-breaker. Practitioner advice for the interim is to hand-check 20 to 30 tags, validate on 500 hand-labelled rows, and route the middle confidence band to a person ([Ryze](https://www.get-ryze.ai/blog/jev-for-marketing)).

## Conclusion

Jev has not shown it can judge content. What it changes is the cost of asking a checkable question, which drops low enough to ask twenty of them about every candidate hook, script line, source and carousel slide. The content engine gains most by turning each brand's taste into a file of explicit, checkable criteria and running it everywhere, and gains nothing from asking any model for a quality score. That rubric file, plus the tags and outcomes accumulated against it, is the durable asset; the scorer behind it is replaceable, and three vendors now sell one.

For a local-first engine this points to a scorer interface with two backends: a local logit-readout model on MLX as the default, and hosted decision models as an optional bring-your-own-key upgrade for accuracy and calibration. Two questions stay open and can only be answered on the engine's own data: whether a local 7B-class model is accurate enough on closed content rubrics, and whether any rubric feature predicts a given brand's results. Both are cheap to test once tags and outcomes are stored side by side.

## Ranked ideas for the content engine

The ranking weighs strength of evidence first, then value and cost to build.

| Rank | Idea | What it needs | Evidence strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Rubric preflight gate.** Run 10 to 20 yes/no checks on every hook, script and carousel slide (names the audience, opens on the payoff, has a concrete number, no banned claim, matches the brief). Block or flag failures. | A per-brand rubric file; a scorer call per item; pinned model version; shuffled option order. | **Strong** for closed rubrics on short text (95.5% faithfulness; ties a frontier judge on RewardBench). No content-specific benchmark. |
| 2 | **Research triage cascade.** Score thousands of sources, comments and competitor posts for relevance; accept confident answers, send the middle band to a generative model or a person. | Per-item requests run concurrently; a confidence threshold; an escalation path; web text treated as untrusted. | **Strong.** The cascade beat a frontier judge at 41% of the cost; Jev out-reranked two local cross-encoders in one test. Escalation rate rises on new tasks. |
| 3 | **Tag, then join to real results.** Tag every published piece and reference piece by hook type, angle and format; join tags to the brand's own performance in plain code; feed surviving patterns back into the rubric. | Stored tags and outcomes per post; minimum-sample rules; analytics import. | **Moderate.** Tagging at scale is shown only in unreproduced demos; the loop is proposed, not run. The method does not depend on the scorer being predictive. |
| 4 | **Pluggable scorer with a local default.** One interface; an MLX logit-readout backend on a permissively licensed model, with hosted decision models as bring-your-own-key options. | A local model of about 7B on 16 GB or more; a small labelled set to measure agreement; a licence check on any distilled clone. | **Moderate.** Mac speeds are measured but self-reported; the local 7B agreed 73.8% vs Jev's 86.6% and is overconfident when wrong. |
| 5 | **Wide hook funnel with forced diversity.** Generate hundreds of hooks and angles, gate them with idea 1, tag them with idea 3, then keep the top few per hook type, not the global top. A person picks from the shortlist. | A generative model for writing; a similarity check against recent posts; some slots reserved for unscored ideas. | **Moderate** for the filter, **weak** for picking winners (zero-shot ranking reaches 0.36 at best; prose-quality judging is at chance). |
| 6 | **Score as prior, bandit as judge.** Post variants, allocate later effort toward what performs, and let outcomes overrule the score. | Variant tracking across posts; outcome import; enough volume per brand. | **Strong** on publisher headline tests; **untested** on organic short-form video. |
| 7 | **Per-brand fitted layer.** Fit a small regularised linear model from the scorer's answer vector to the brand's outcomes. | Low hundreds of posts with outcomes; chance-corrected evaluation. | **Weak to moderate.** Embedding regressions alone were barely better than random; useful only as a prior for idea 6. |
| 8 | **Still-frame and thumbnail checks.** Ask closed questions of sampled frames (text legible, subject visible, brand colours present). | Image input on a hosted model, or a local vision model. | **Weak.** Image input is new and labelled beta by one host; no independent evaluation found. |

Two ideas are ranked out on the evidence: simulated persona panels, which performed worse than a single plain prompt, and any cold "virality" or "quality" score, for which no supporting evidence exists.
