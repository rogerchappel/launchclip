# Halcyon 2, explained: what a frontier model launch actually changes

## The launch nobody saw coming

On a quiet Tuesday in March, Northlight Labs shipped a model that nobody had on their calendar. There was no keynote, no countdown, and no teaser trailer. There was a blog post, a forty page system card, and a pull request that quietly changed the default model in their public SDK. Within six hours, the post had been shared more than ninety thousand times. Within two days, developers had pushed over four thousand projects that called the new model by name. So what is Halcyon 2, why did it land so hard, and what does it actually change for the people who build with it? That is what this video is about. We will look at the numbers Northlight published, the numbers they did not publish, and the evidence from developers who tested it in the first week. By the end, you will know which claims hold up, which ones are marketing, and what this launch tells us about the next twelve months. Let's start with what was actually announced.

## A short history of Halcyon

To see why this release matters, it helps to know where Halcyon came from. The first Halcyon model shipped in 2023, as a research preview that only a few hundred partners could use. It was slow, expensive, and very good at one thing, which was reading long contracts. In 2024, Northlight released Halcyon 1.5, which added code, images and a public API. That version was the one most developers met. It was based on the same architecture as the original, with more data and a longer training run. Then, in 2025, the company went quiet. No new models, a few research papers, and a hiring spree in Finland and Canada. Looking back, that quiet year was the training run for Halcyon 2. Each version descended from the one before, but the jump from 1.5 to 2 is the largest in the family. The original model could read about fifty pages at a time. Version 1.5 could read about five hundred. Version 2 can read roughly a thousand. That lineage tells you what Northlight optimises for. Every release pushed the same idea further: read more, remember more, and finish longer tasks without losing the thread.

## Inside the training run

Training a model like this is mostly a story about hardware. The new cluster holds about 30 thousand accelerators, arranged in racks of 64, linked by a network that moves data between them at enormous speed. Running it costs real money. Outside analysts estimate the training run alone cost between 400 million and 600 million dollars, once you count the chips, the power and the people. Northlight has not confirmed a number. Power is the other constraint. The Finnish site was chosen because electricity there is cheap, mostly renewable, and the cold climate makes cooling easier. The system card says the run used about 120 gigawatt hours of electricity, roughly what a town of ten thousand homes uses in a year. Then there is the data. The card describes three sources: public web text, licensed books and articles, and synthetic data generated by earlier models and checked by people. The share of synthetic data rose from about 10 percent to about 35 percent. That is the quiet trend across the whole industry. Models are increasingly trained on material that other models wrote, and the hard part is checking it.

## What Northlight actually announced

The announcement made three claims. First, Halcyon 2 is faster, answering a typical request in about 1.8 seconds instead of 4.1 seconds. Second, it is cheaper, at 3 dollars per million input tokens, down from 8 dollars. Third, it is better at long tasks, finishing 62 percent of multi step coding problems on their internal benchmark, compared with 41 percent for the previous model. Those are big jumps for a single release. Faster, cheaper and better usually do not arrive together, because each one normally costs you one of the others. The system card fills in some of the detail. Halcyon 2 was trained on a new cluster in Finland, using roughly 30 thousand accelerators over eleven weeks. The context window doubled, from 200 thousand tokens to 400 thousand. And for the first time, Northlight published a short section on what the model still gets wrong, including a table of known failure cases. That table matters more than it looks, and we will come back to it. But first, the speed claim, because that is the one most people noticed.

## Why speed changed everything

Speed sounds like the boring part of a launch. It is not. When a model answers in four seconds, people use it like a search box. They ask, they wait, and they read. When it answers in under two seconds, people start using it like a keyboard. They let it finish their sentences, fix their code as they type, and suggest the next step before they ask. Northlight's own chart shows median response time falling from 4.1 seconds to 1.8 seconds, and the slowest tenth of requests falling from 11 seconds to 4 seconds. That second number is the one developers felt. The slow tail is what makes a tool feel broken. Independent testers mostly agreed. One open benchmark run by a group of volunteers measured 2.1 seconds on average across three regions, a little slower than the claim, but close. Another developer posted a side by side recording of the old model and the new one completing the same task. The new one finished before the old one had printed its first line. That clip was viewed more than two million times. Speed is the claim that holds up best, and it is the one that changes behaviour most.

## The context window, doubled

The context window is how much text the model can hold in mind at once, measured in tokens. A token is a piece of a word, so a page of English is roughly five hundred tokens. Halcyon 2 doubled its window, from 200 thousand tokens to 400 thousand, which is roughly eight hundred pages. In practice, that means you can hand it an entire codebase, a full legal case file, or a year of meeting notes, and ask questions across all of it. But a bigger window is only useful if the model actually uses it. The standard test hides a single fact deep inside a long document and asks the model to find it. On that test, Halcyon 2 found the fact 97 percent of the time at full length, compared with 84 percent for the previous model. That is a real improvement. The harder test asks the model to connect two facts that are hundreds of pages apart. There, the score drops to about 70 percent. So the window is bigger, and the model reads it well, but reasoning across the whole thing is still hard. Long context is a capability, not a guarantee.

## The price cut and who pays for it

Now the price. Three dollars per million input tokens is a cut of more than sixty percent. For a small team running a support bot, that can turn a monthly bill of 2,400 dollars into roughly 900 dollars. For a large company, the savings run into millions. But a price cut has to come from somewhere. There are three places it can come from: cheaper hardware, a smaller model, or thinner margins. Northlight says it is mostly the first. The new cluster uses a newer generation of chips, and the system card reports that serving cost per token fell by about half. That accounts for a lot of the cut, but not all of it. The rest is probably margin, and that is a strategic choice. Northlight is betting that more usage today means more customers tomorrow. Competitors noticed. Within ten days, two rival labs announced their own price cuts, of 40 percent and 50 percent. So the real story of the price cut is not one cheaper model. It is a price war, and developers are the ones who win it, at least for now.

## Reading the benchmarks honestly

Benchmarks are where launches get slippery. Northlight reported 62 percent on its own coding benchmark, up from 41 percent. That is a large jump, but it is their benchmark, built from their own problems, and scored by their own graders. The more useful numbers come from public benchmarks that other people run. On the open software engineering test that most labs report, Halcyon 2 scored 48 percent, compared with 39 percent for the previous model and 51 percent for the best rival. So it improved, but it did not take the top spot. On a long document reading test, it scored 71 percent, which does lead, by about four points. And on a maths test for high school competitions, it scored lower than the previous version, falling from 58 percent to 55 percent. Northlight mentioned that drop in a footnote. The honest summary is this. Halcyon 2 is a clear step up on long tasks and long documents, a modest step on coding, and a small step back on maths. That is a normal, healthy release. It is not a revolution, and the chart in the announcement made it look like one.

## What developers found in week one

The best evidence came from developers, in public, in the first week. On GitHub, the pull request that switched the SDK default collected more than six hundred comments. Some were celebrations. Many were bug reports. The most upvoted comment pointed out that the new model ignored a formatting setting that the old one respected, which broke a popular command line tool overnight. Northlight merged a fix four days later, in a follow up commit that restored the old behaviour behind a flag. On social media, the reactions split into two camps. One camp posted demos: an agent that refactored a whole codebase, a tutor that explained calculus step by step, and a game built from a single prompt. The other camp posted failures: confident wrong answers, invented citations, and a widely shared example where the model counted the letters in a word incorrectly, three times in a row. Both camps were right. The model is better at long, structured work, and still unreliable at small, exact tasks. If you build with it, you want tests around every answer that has to be exactly right.

## The failure table and what it admits

Remember the table of known failure cases in the system card. It lists twelve categories of mistakes, with an estimated rate for each. The highest rate is for questions about events after the training cutoff, where the model guesses wrongly about 30 percent of the time. Next is arithmetic on long numbers, at about 18 percent. Then come citations, where roughly one in ten references the model produces cannot be found. Publishing a table like this is unusual. Most launches show only what improved. A failure table tells you where the model is weak, so you can design around it. It also gives outside researchers something to check. Within a week, an independent group reproduced four of the twelve rows and found rates within two points of Northlight's numbers. That is a good sign. It means the company measured honestly, at least on the parts that were checked. The lesson for anyone shipping a product on top of a model is simple. Read the failure table first, the benchmark chart second, and the launch video last.

## How the rivals answered

The rest of the industry responded quickly. Two days after the launch, the largest rival lab cut its prices by 40 percent and published a blog post comparing its own model favourably on maths. Five days after the launch, a second lab released an updated model with a faster mode, claiming 1.5 seconds per response. Ten days after the launch, an open source group published a smaller model that matched Halcyon 1.5 on most public benchmarks and could run on a single workstation. On the public leaderboard that ranks models by blind human votes, the order shifted three times in two weeks. Halcyon 2 entered at second place, briefly took first, then settled at third after the rival updates. The spread between first and fifth place is now smaller than it has ever been. That is the real competitive picture. No single lab is far ahead. Each release buys a few weeks of attention, and then the others catch up. For developers, that means switching costs matter more than loyalty. If your product can swap one model for another in an afternoon, you can always use the best one.

## What this means for the next year

So what does Halcyon 2 change? Three things. First, speed now matters as much as intelligence. Tools that respond in under two seconds will feel different, and users will notice. Second, prices are falling fast. If your product was too expensive to run last year, run the numbers again this year. Third, honest documentation is becoming a competitive feature. A lab that publishes its failure rates earns trust that a chart cannot buy. Looking ahead, expect rivals to answer with faster models of their own before the end of the summer. Expect at least one more round of price cuts. And expect the gap between the top labs to keep shrinking, measured in weeks rather than years. If you build with these models, the practical advice is the same as ever. Measure on your own tasks, keep tests around exact answers, and do not trust a launch chart you cannot reproduce.
